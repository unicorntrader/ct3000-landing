Trading discipline

Why most traders overtrade

5 min read

Overtrading almost never feels like a mistake while it's happening. It feels like opportunity. There's a setup, then another setup, then a third one that's "even better" — and by the end of the session you've taken twelve trades instead of the three your plan actually called for. Nobody sits down intending to overtrade. It just accumulates, one reasonable-sounding decision at a time.

It's also not a beginner problem that goes away with experience. Paul Tudor Jones — one of the more famous macro traders of the last forty years — has been open about an early-career blowup, and pinned the cause not on being wrong about a trade, but on position size relative to his account: he was running far more contracts than his equity justified. Being wrong wasn't what hurt him. Being wrong at that size was.

"The most important rule of trading is to play great defense, not great offense." — Paul Tudor Jones

That's the lesson overtrading teaches everyone eventually, one way or another: trading is a business, and businesses that survive are the ones with disciplined cost control, not the ones swinging hardest on every deal.

Why it's hard to catch in the moment

Overtrading is invisible from inside a single session. Each individual trade can look defensible — a real setup, a real reason. What's missing from the in-the-moment view is the aggregate: how many trades you've taken today relative to your usual pace, how your win rate degrades on trade six versus trade one, whether your average size has been creeping up without a matching increase in conviction.

None of that shows up on a single ticket. It only shows up in the data, after the fact, across enough trades to see the shape of it.
